Description of the project and position

This PhD project investigates how high-fat diet exposure disrupts the small intestinal barrier and contributes to the development of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Using advanced murine models, human intestinal organoids, and intestinal endothelial cell cultures, the project aims to unravel the molecular mechanisms underlying diet-induced barrier dysfunction, with a particular focus on β-adrenergic signaling and epithelial and endothelial permeability. Combining in vivo experiments with functional barrier assays, imaging, and molecular analyses, the project will explore how dietary fat alters intestinal barrier integrity and function and may identify novel therapeutic targets to prevent gut barrier dysfunction and its contribution to metabolic and liver disease. Studies will be conducted in the Department of BioMedical Research (DBMR), in the research laboratories of the Gastroenterology/Mucosal Immunology division. We offer a highly interdisciplinary research environment dedicated to the study of mucosal immunology, microbiota, inflammation, and cancer development, involving human work, animal studies, and computational biology, besides close supervision and a great team spirit. Payment is based on the Swiss National Science Foundation (SNSF)'s funding regulations for PhD students.

Description

The Wiest group at the Division of Gastroenterology and Mucosal Immunology at the Department for BioMedical Research at the University of Bern is looking for a PhD student to work on a project on High-fat-diet (HFD) induced disruption of instestinal barriers. Our group is interested in the mechanisms leading to intestinal barrier breakage, pathological bacterial translocation in obesity and gut-liver interactions in chronic liver disease, using a wide range of techniques and model systems ranging from mouse models, via intestinal organoid cultures to human cohorts (Castillo et al, 2026, PMID: 42521089 Wyss et al, 2026, PMID: 41999240). The work will be conducted in close collaboration with the group of Prof. Stephanie Ganal-Vonarburg, who investigates the impact of dietary and microbial signals on intestinal and immune development in early life.
